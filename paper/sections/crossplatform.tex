\section{RQ5: Cross-platform replication}
\label{sec:crossplatform}

The Jetson study traces the in-app cost to an accelerator governor
that reacts to the host pipeline's duty cycle, coupled to a CPU
governor that reacts to how the host waits. To test whether this is
board-specific, the core experiments were repeated on two boards with
different accelerator architectures (\cref{tab:boards}): a Raspberry
Pi~5 with a Hailo-8, a fixed-function INT8 NPU with on-chip NMS and no
user-visible frequency control, and a Rubik Pi~3 (Qualcomm QCM6490),
measured on its CPU under a three-cluster \texttt{schedutil} DVFS and
on its Hexagon HTP NPU through a separately obtained QNN runtime. Both
follow the protocol of \cref{sec:setup}: official COCOeval on val2017
(all 5,000 images for accuracy; the ladder uses the first 1,000 on the
slower Rubik), the same letterbox geometry and row conversion, hashed
predictions, and a rotated two-repeat campaign. Logs are sampled at
20\,Hz on the Jetson, 10\,Hz on the Pi (the rate its PMIC sustains)
and 13--18\,Hz on the Rubik, which logs clocks and thermals only. The
pinned-frequency probes and the HTP streaming cells are single runs;
both are flagged where they appear.

\subsection{Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-8 accelerator}
\label{sec:pi}

The Hailo model-zoo YOLOv8n HEF offloads the whole network, including
NMS, to the NPU; the host decodes, letterboxes into a uint8 NHWC
buffer and converts the NMS output to COCO rows. The vendor benchmark
(\texttt{hailortcli benchmark}) reports 431.6 images/s in throughput
mode under both CPU governors, while a conventional sequential
application on the same HEF reaches 15\% of that (\cref{tab:piladder}).
The NPU clock is neither user-controllable nor exposed by any
register, so an internal NPU DVFS can be neither ruled in nor ruled
out.

The ladder mechanisms transfer (\cref{tab:piladder}). Prefetching
decode gives $1.86\times$; overlapping post-processing with a second
decode worker gives another $1.5\times$; a third worker adds nothing,
as on the Jetson. The restructuring reaches $2.7\times$ the sequential
application ($3.3\times$ with the spinning wait). Predictions are
byte-identical in every rung, repeat and governor (sha256 prefix
\texttt{455ea22b07f1}; official AP 0.320, AP$_S$ 0.122 on all 5,000
images).

The wait-policy effect also appears, through a different mechanism.
Replacing the sleeping wait on the asynchronous inference handle with
a spinning wait gains 22\% under \texttt{ondemand} and 29\% under
\texttt{performance}. Under \texttt{performance} the CPU clock is
pinned at 2.4\,GHz, so the CPU governor cannot mediate the gain; it
sits inside HailoRT's wait and callback path, which was not profiled
further. A separate, smaller governor effect exists: a controlled
probe (\texttt{seq} versus \texttt{seq} with a busy spinner) shows the
governor holding 2.4\,GHz instead of 2.2\,GHz, worth 7.7\% of
throughput at 42\% more energy. The NPU time from submission to
completion stays at 6.3--9.3\,ms across the ladder; the wait inside
that interval absorbs the schedule differences.

Energy per image falls 1.8$\times$ from sequential to the best variant.
These figures cover only the SoC rails (\cref{tab:boards}); the
unmeasured NPU energy can itself move with the schedule, so they do
not establish whole-system rankings. SoC-side
comparisons remain meaningful between configurations sharing the same
engine and accuracy; cross-engine comparisons on this board need
module-level power measurement. Streaming at 15, 30 and 60\,fps is
consistent with the Jetson's idle-floor pattern
(\cref{tab:streamreplication}): sequential and overlapped pipelines
differ by 9\% in energy per frame at 15\,fps and converge at 60\,fps.
The 15\,fps gap is indicative only, because its overlapped cell has a
19\% two-repeat spread (101--121\,mJ) and the sequential pipeline
already sits at the idle floor within measurement error (single 60\,s
idle window: $1.534$\,W $\Rightarrow$ 102.3\,mJ per frame at 15\,fps).

Two differences from the Jetson matter. First, accuracy is not
comparable across the boards. The HEF's on-chip NMS is compiled with a
score threshold of 0.2, truncating the low-score tail that COCO AP
rewards: AP is 0.320, against the 0.373 that Ultralytics reports for
the same network at the conventional 0.001
threshold~\cite{ultralytics2023yolov8}. The threshold explains the
direction and plausibly most of the magnitude; without pre-threshold
rows, smaller contributions from the HEF toolchain cannot be ruled
out. Because every rung produces byte-identical predictions, this
cancels in within-platform comparisons. Second, the default
\texttt{ondemand} governor costs less than \texttt{nvhost\_podgov} on
the Jetson: it sits at 2.0\,GHz even in the sequential pipeline and,
once the pipeline is overlapped, nearly matches \texttt{performance}
(219.8 versus 217.5\,images/s at the best rung). The Jetson's
threefold governor effect is specific to its aggressive GPU governor;
what generalizes is that the benchmark number describes a duty cycle
the application creates, not a property of the accelerator. One
anomaly is unexplained by clocks: on the overlap rungs
\texttt{ondemand} is 5--6\% faster than \texttt{performance}, while
with the spinning wait the order inverts (\cref{tab:piladder});
NPU-side power states, not pursued here, may differ.

\subsection{Rubik Pi 3: inference on the CPU}
\label{sec:rubik}

The Rubik Pi~3 runs the Jetson float engine's YOLO26n ONNX graph on
the CPU (ONNX Runtime, default threads), so inference competes with
the host stages and the wait policy for the same resource. In the
overlap rungs each decode worker runs the session call, so up to
\texttt{max\_workers} inferences execute concurrently and the
\emph{infer} column includes their contention. The frequency probes
pin the run to the prime core (\texttt{taskset}, intra-op parallelism
1) to exercise a single frequency domain; the ladder runs are
unpinned. On all 5,000 val2017 images the board reproduces the Jetson
engine's AP almost exactly (0.4027 versus 0.40257, AP$_S$ 0.1977
versus 0.19766). Multithreaded inference emits rows in a
nondeterministic order, so predictions are compared after
canonicalization (sorting rows by image, category, score and box): all
six rungs produce the same 143,362 canonical rows on the 1,000-image
subset in every repeat and governor (24 of 24 cells), and
\texttt{seq}, \texttt{full\_w2} and \texttt{full\_w2\_spin} give
identical AP (0.425).

Every effect that dominated on the accelerator boards shrinks or
inverts (\cref{tab:rubikladder}). Prefetch alone no longer helps
($-5$\%), because the decode worker steals a core from inference.
Overlap with one worker is best, but gains only $+28$\% and nearly
triples the median latency, because the
queued frame waits behind a 400\,ms inference. The spinning wait,
worth 20--22\% on both accelerator boards, gains nothing, since the
spinner competes with the inference threads for cores. The two
governors differ by less than the drift between identical repeats,
consistent with both sitting at the thermal limit. The SoC reached
95.4--95.8\,$^\circ$C, the firmware ceiling, in every ladder run, and
a late repeat is up to 17\% slower than an earlier identical one; the
Jetson's median run-to-run range was 0.8\%. No hardware throttle
counter was read, so the drift is attributed to thermal management by
its signature (sustained ceiling temperature plus progressive
slowdown), not by direct observation.

What remains controllable is the clock. Pinning the big core's
frequency domain to 806, 1862 and 2707\,MHz moves the sequential
pipeline through 1.7, 3.1 and 3.8\,images/s, and inference time from
575.7 to 255.2\,ms, a $2.26\times$ ratio for a $3.36\times$ frequency
ratio. Each pin is a single run and the other two domains float, so
this is a dose--response, not a scaling law. Frequency is a direct
throughput control here, because the pipeline never idles the
processor long enough to confuse \texttt{schedutil}. In streaming (2,
5 and 10\,fps) both pipelines keep up only at 2\,fps, where the
sequential variant again wins on latency (p50 290 versus 356\,ms) and
the big core drops to 2.3--2.5\,GHz in the idle gaps. At 5 and
10\,fps the queue grows linearly: within a 300-frame stream the
sequential median latency reaches 23.9 and 39.3\,s, and the
overlapped pipeline's higher capacity (3.8 versus 2.8\,images/s) holds
it to 8.6 and 26.2\,s at a saturated 2.7\,GHz. The sequential capacity
is below the ladder's 3.26 because the streaming phase ran late, when
the board was hottest. No schedule rescues a pipeline whose capacity
is below the arrival rate; here the clock, or a cheaper model, is the
only lever.

\subsection{Rubik Pi 3 on its Hexagon NPU}
\label{sec:rubikhtp}

The QCM6490's intended accelerator is a Hexagon HTP (V68) NPU. The
board's QNN runtime predates the available INT8 context binary of the
same YOLO26n graph (\cref{tab:boards}), and the newer SDK requires a
vendor account. The \texttt{qai-appbuilder} PyPI wheel bundles a
matching \texttt{libQnnHtp} and V68 skel; with the DSP (digital signal
processor) library path pointed at the wheel, the context runs
unchanged. The context, not the pipeline, brings two
protocol differences: it is INT8, and it ends in the standard
$(1{,}84{,}8400)$ head, so NMS runs on the host with the reference val
settings (confidence ${\ge}\,0.001$, IoU $0.7$, at most 300
detections). It scores AP $0.364$ (AP$_S$ $0.175$) on all 5,000 images
against $0.4027$ ($0.198$) for the float CPU stack, trading $3.9$
points of AP for eleven times the throughput of the best CPU pipeline
under the same governor (43.6 versus 4.07\,images/s under
\texttt{performance}; $6.7\times$ under \texttt{schedutil}).

One caveat applies to that comparison. The context's uint8 score
encoding has a quantization scale of $0.01045$ (measured on the output
grid), so the effective score floor is ${\approx}0.01$ rather than
$0.001$: the Hailo mechanism, twenty-fold milder. It truncates only
the far tail; on fewer than ten of the 5,000 images the stack emits no
rows, all of them images whose float-stack detections score below
$0.02$. A sensitivity check quantizes the float stack's scores to
$0.01$ steps and drops the sub-$0.01$ tail, which lowers its AP from
$0.4026$ to $0.3999$ ($-0.27$ points, AP$_S$ $-0.5$). The score floor
therefore explains about 7\% of the 3.9-point gap. The check covers
only the score floor, not the quantization of boxes or features or the
stacks' different error distributions, so it does not apportion the
remaining gap. Within the NPU stack, predictions are byte-identical
across every rung, repeat and governor (sha256 prefix
\texttt{09de71d67bad} on the ladder subset), and the accuracy run's
rows for those images match row for row. Each accuracy row is a single
run; COCOeval is deterministic, so a repeat could differ only through
runtime nondeterminism.

The bundled appbuilder/QNN userspace wedged the DSP session under
concurrent multi-context inference, so the pipeline holds exactly one
context and issues every NPU call from a single thread; overlap comes
from pipelining decode, input fill and host NMS \emph{around} the
serialized NPU calls. Under that discipline no call failed. Separately,
an early evaluation-script bug inflated every timed call by a per-call
error print; it was fixed, the entire HTP campaign was rerun, and only
corrected numbers are reported. \Cref{app:qnn} details both issues and
why the bug mattered most where clock sensitivity is examined.

\Cref{tab:rubikhtpladder} shows a return to the accelerator pattern.
With a 24--28\,ms NPU call, the host stages are again the tax, and the
ladder recovers them: $+24$\% under \texttt{schedutil} (22.9 to
28.5\,images/s, the spinning-wait diagnostic; the best non-spinning
rung reaches 27.8) and $+52$\% under \texttt{performance} (28.8 to
43.6\,images/s). Under \texttt{performance} the best rung saturates
the accelerator: its 22.9\,ms period matches the loaded per-call
service time, marginally below the 24.4\,ms isolated-call median. That
median implies about 41 calls/s, which the best rung exceeds, so it
overstates the loaded service time. Under sustained
submission either queued calls overlap driver-side work or the loaded
dispatch share shrinks; the two cannot be separated.

Because NPU calls are serialized, a second decode worker does not pay
off. Under \texttt{performance} \texttt{full\_w2} is slower than
\texttt{full\_w1} and adds 39\,ms of p50 latency, since the extra
worker only lengthens the queue; under \texttt{schedutil} the two tie,
because the slowed feeder core binds either way. The governor,
irrelevant on the CPU board, is the largest single term here. Under
\texttt{schedutil} the core that feeds the NPU settles at
$0.95$--$1.0$\,GHz in the overlapped rungs and at $1.78$\,GHz in the
sequential one: the less host work a rung leaves on the critical path,
the lower the clock the governor chooses. The same pipeline runs up to
57\% faster under \texttt{performance} (43.6 versus 27.8\,images/s on
the matched \texttt{full\_w1} rung; 44\% on the matched
\texttt{full\_w2} rung, 39.9 versus 27.7\,images/s).

The pinned-frequency probe shows why the host clock matters for an
``NPU'' call. At 806, 1862 and 2707\,MHz the sequential pipeline
delivers 16.7, 24.0 and 28.6\,images/s, and the measured NPU time
falls from 35.8 to 24.4\,ms. A least-squares fit decomposes the three
points into a fixed ${\approx}20$\,ms NPU kernel plus a host-side
dispatch term inversely proportional to the pinned core's clock, so a
large share of each call is remote-procedure-call (RPC) work on the
CPU. The decomposition is descriptive, not a validated model: the mid
point sits $0.8$\,ms above the fit, and two parameters over three
single-run pins leave one residual degree of freedom. With no
observable NPU clock or load register, it also cannot distinguish a
clock-invariant kernel from an accelerator DVFS absorbed by the
constant term. The governor invariance below is therefore a hypothesis
consistent with the data, not a measurement.

The spinning wait gains nothing here (38.2 versus 39.9\,images/s under
\texttt{performance}): a single serial caller has no
asynchronous-handle wait for the spinner to heat. This cell also shows
the campaign's widest repeat spread (34.9--41.5\,images/s), which is
thermal drift, with the SoC climbing from 83 to 95\,$^\circ$C, not
noise. Within the decomposition, what moves with the governor is the
call's host-dispatch share, not the accelerator clock: the sequential
call takes 24.4\,ms under \texttt{performance} and 28.3\,ms under
\texttt{schedutil}, while the fitted kernel is governor-invariant.
Under the shipped governor the serialized call path at the best rung delivers one completion every 35.1\,ms, about 24\%
slower than the 28.3\,ms sequential-call rate, even when the queue
never empties. The \emph{infer} column rises to ${\approx}47$\,ms in
the overlap rungs because it then includes the queue wait behind the
serialized caller, not because the accelerator slows.

Streaming behaves as the capacities predict
(\cref{tab:streamreplication}; single runs, so the governor contrast
is paired but not replicated). Under \texttt{schedutil} the pipeline
keeps up at 15\,fps (p50 108\,ms overlapped, 54\,ms sequential) and
queues linearly at 30 and 60\,fps (p50 0.8 and 5.1\,s overlapped, 2.4
and 6.7\,s sequential). Under \texttt{performance} the overlapped
pipeline sustains 30\,fps at p50 47\,ms, while the sequential one
(capacity 28.8\,images/s) already falls behind (p50 0.39\,s); at
60\,fps both queue (p50 2.1 versus 4.7\,s). Predictions agree row for
row with the ladder on the shared images.

\paragraph{Baking selection into the graph}
The Jetson and Rubik CPU stacks fuse selection into the NMS-free
$(1{,}300{,}6)$ head, and the Hailo HEF runs NMS on-chip, so an
end-to-end context was also built for the HTP with the matching QAIRT
2.50.40 toolchain (INT8, 500 calibration images, context binary
generated on the board). One silent pitfall arose: the exported head
routes the box branch (range ${\approx}\,[-50,800]$) and the score
branch ($[0,1]$) through a single concat--transpose--split, and
per-tensor INT8 quantization gives the shared tensor one scale, which
zeroes the score channel at runtime. Graph surgery, not calibration,
fixes this: the branches are transposed separately, and boxes, scores
and classes are exposed as three output tensors with their own
scales. Mixed precision is not an option, because the V68 HTP rejects
fp16 graphs outright.

The repaired context detects correctly and is faster end to end,
because selection moves onto the DSP and host NMS disappears: on this
toolchain it runs 45\% faster at peak (\Cref{tab:rubikhtpe2e}), and
under \texttt{performance} the overlapped 30\,fps stream cuts its p50
latency by 42\% (27 versus 47\,ms). The same descriptive decomposition
puts the fixed NPU kernel at ${\approx}11.6$\,ms, against
${\approx}20$\,ms for the standard head (endpoint and least-squares
fits span 11.5--11.6\,ms, with the mid probe point $0.3$\,ms above the
fit). The fused head scores 7 AP points lower, with the loss
concentrated in small objects, where the in-graph INT8 box-decode chain
(regression logits at $0.75$ per step, decoded boxes at $3.3$\,px per
step) hurts most. The standard head with host NMS therefore remains
the primary HTP stack. Because the two contexts differ in QAIRT release
and calibration set, this is a configuration comparison, not an
isolated cost of fusing selection.

\subsection{What transfers, and what does not}
\label{sec:transfer}

The \emph{benchmark-versus-application gap} appears on all four
stacks: 15\% of the benchmark on the Pi~5, 4.2 versus 13.2\,ms on the
Jetson, a 254\,ms inference inside a 304\,ms loop on the Rubik CPU,
and a 28\,ms NPU call inside a 43\,ms loop on the HTP under the
shipped governor. What fills the gap differs by stack, as summarized at the end of this
section.

The \emph{host-pipeline ladder} helps on all four stacks, by an amount
that depends on what else moves with the schedule (best rung versus
sequential, shipped governor). On the Jetson it
reaches $6.3\times$ against the naive sequential rung (float input,
stream synchronization) and $4.3$--$5.3\times$ against the stronger
sequential variants of \cref{tab:ladder}, because overlap also lifts
the GPU out of its DVFS floor; the $2.4\times$ headline of
\cref{sec:intro} uses the locked-clock baseline. The gain is
$3.3\times$ on the Pi, the most host-bound of the four, and
$1.3\times$ on the Rubik CPU. On the HTP it is only $1.2\times$,
because \texttt{schedutil} settles the feeder core near 1\,GHz once the
pipeline overlaps; fixing the governor first re-opens it to
$1.5\times$ (\Cref{tab:rubikhtpladder}).

The \emph{wait-policy effect} appears on both asynchronous-handle
accelerators, through different mechanisms. On the Jetson the spinning
wait gains 20--25\% on the sequential and prefetch rungs, mediated by
the CPU governor (\cref{sec:sched}), and nothing on the full
two-worker pipeline, whose host never idles on the critical path. On
the Pi it gains 22--29\% on the \emph{best} rung and persists at a
pinned CPU clock, so it lives in the runtime's wait path. It vanishes
where no such wait exists: on the Rubik CPU the spinner steals cycles
from inference, and the HTP's single serial caller waits on a bounded
queue rather than an async handle.

The \emph{accelerator governor}, the Jetson's dominant term, is
observable only where the accelerator exposes a clock. Neither NPU
does; an internal DVFS cannot be ruled out, but none is needed to
explain the measurements. The measured per-call NPU time includes
host-side wait and runtime queueing (it grows from 6.3 to 9.3\,ms
across the Pi's ladder; the HTP's infer column spans queue waits), and
once that share is accounted for, neither stack shows a residual that
moves with the host duty cycle. The governor story moves rather than
disappears: on the HTP the \emph{CPU} governor is the largest term
(57\% on the matched \texttt{full\_w1} rungs), because
\texttt{schedutil} down-clocks the feeder core and each call's
host-dispatch share grows as that clock falls (\cref{sec:rubikhtp}).

\emph{Thermal limits} bind on the Rubik CPU stack, which sits at the
95\,$^\circ$C ceiling with measurable repeat drift. The NPU stacks
approach the same ceiling under \texttt{performance}
(91--95\,$^\circ$C). The Pi shows no drift; the two heaviest
\texttt{performance} overlap rungs on the HTP do (\texttt{full\_w2\_spin}
and \texttt{full\_w3}, which falls from 42.6 to 37.5\,images/s between
repeats), so these two cells mix governor and thermal effects; the
\texttt{schedutil} rows and both sequential ladders show no drift.

\emph{Streaming} follows capacity on the NPU boards as
\cref{sec:stream} predicts (\Cref{tab:streamreplication}): the Pi
keeps up at every rate, since its sequential capacity already exceeds
60\,fps, and the HTP queues precisely when the arrival rate passes the
ladder capacity. The Jetson's plain-overlap hold of each frame's rows
for about one arrival period (\cref{sec:stream}) belongs to the
harness's double-buffered host loop and does not occur on either NPU
stack. HailoRT delivers results through an asynchronous completion
callback, so the Pi's overlapped p50 tracks the service time
(${\approx}17$\,ms) at every rate, and the HTP pipeline submits rows
as soon as the serialized call returns. On the HTP the shipped
governor inflates latency instead, because the slowed feeder core
stretches both the call and the overlapping host NMS (15\,fps
overlapped cells).

For a practitioner, the ordering of \cref{sec:discussion} survives
with two amendments. On CPU-inference devices, stop after decode
overlap with a single worker and spend the effort on clocks and
cooling. On NPU devices, check the CPU governor early: on the QNN/HTP
stack it was the largest single term, while the Hailo stack's shipped
governor was already within 1\% of the pinned one at the best rung.

\textbf{Answer to RQ5.}
The findings carry over in outline, not in mechanism. On all four
stacks (Jetson TensorRT, Hailo-8, Rubik CPU, Rubik HTP), isolated
engine or inference-call measurements understate the end-to-end
application cost, for different reasons: a GPU-governor-induced
engine slowdown on the Jetson (\cref{sec:diagnosis}), host and runtime
overhead around a fixed-function NPU on the Pi, shared CPU resources
and thermal saturation on the Rubik CPU, and the CPU-side dispatch
share of each call and its response to the CPU governor on the HTP.
In each case the host-pipeline ladder recovers throughput without
touching the engine, and streaming queues exactly when the arrival
rate passes the ladder-measured capacity. Across model architectures
(\cref{sec:generality}), YOLOv10n and YOLO26s (whose engine takes
$1.6\times$ longer per image) reproduce the same schedule effects
($5.6$--$6.9\times$ over the Ultralytics predictor), so the
conclusions are not specific to YOLO26n.
