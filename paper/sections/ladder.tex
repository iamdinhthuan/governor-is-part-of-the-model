\textbf{Accuracy.}
Our uint8 pipeline scores 0.40257 AP and 0.19766 AP$_S$, against 0.40259
and 0.19779 for the Ultralytics predictor on its own engine. The
float-input variant scores 0.40240. For reference, the YOLO26 paper
reports 40.9 AP for YOLO26n, measured on a desktop GPU with its own
export~\cite{jocher2026yolo26}; on our device and evaluation settings
the Ultralytics predictor itself scores 0.40259, so the
${\approx}0.6$-point difference from the paper is a property of the
evaluation stack, not of our pipeline. Reaching this agreement required one
fix to our decoder. An earlier version subtracted the fractional
letterbox padding, rather than the integer offset actually used to place
the resized image. This shifted boxes by half a pixel whenever the
padding was odd and measurably lowered AP on the verification split. Such errors are easy to make and hard to notice in a custom
pipeline, so we checked every pipeline in this paper with official
COCOeval on the full split.

\textbf{Baselines.}
Under default governors the Ultralytics predictor processes 31.1
images/s at 194.6\,mJ per image. Our float-input sequential pipeline is
similar (33.9 images/s). Both run the GPU at 306\,MHz for the whole run.
Locking clocks raises them to 73.9 and 59.9 images/s, at 3.5--4.2\,W more
module input power. Locking clocks with \texttt{jetson\_clocks} is the usual recommendation for benchmarking on Jetson~\cite{nvidia2024jetsonperf}.

\textbf{Host-side changes help a sequential schedule only through the host.}
Moving normalization and the channel transpose into the engine saves
4.1\,ms of CPU time per image and raises throughput by 18\% (33.9 to
40.1 images/s). Waiting on an event instead of the stream raises it to 50.0 images/s; put the other way, the stream-synchronized pipeline loses 20\% of throughput. The GPU still runs at 306\,MHz and the
engine still takes 13.2\,ms. The event-sync gain comes from the host
side, at a cost of 1.3\,W and 3.7\,ms of CPU time per image
(\cref{sec:sched}).

\textbf{Overlapping CPU and GPU work changes the governor's regime.}
Prefetching the next image's decode and letterbox gives the largest
single step, from 50.0 to 146.6 images/s. The GPU is now idle only
briefly between inferences, and the governor raises its mean clock to
911\,MHz. The engine's enqueue-to-completion time falls from 13.2 to
4.4\,ms, close to its back-to-back value. Overlapping row construction
of image $i{-}1$ with inference of image $i$ gives 175.2 images/s at
999\,MHz. A second decode worker gives 213.8 images/s at 1019\,MHz. More workers do not help. In a separate sweep over one to five workers (two runs each), throughput was 174.9, 214.2, 215.9, 216.4 and 216.6 images/s, so the third to fifth workers add 1.1\% together. With two workers the GPU's measured load is already 0.90 (worker-sweep session; 0.87 in the load-logging session of \cref{sec:diagnosis}) and its clock is at the maximum, so the pipeline is limited by the engine and the main thread, not by decode. Extra workers only lengthen the queue: median latency per image (from the image entering the pipeline to its finished rows) grows from 17.2\,ms with two workers to 30.1\,ms with five. The full pipeline is $6.9\times$ faster than the Ultralytics predictor under the same default governors. The Ultralytics predictor is a weak baseline, so we also compare against the strongest sequential configuration we measured, uint8 input with event synchronization and all clocks locked (90.4 images/s). The full pipeline under default governors is still $2.4\times$ faster, at 0.4026 AP in both cases.

\textbf{Locking clocks becomes unnecessary.}
With locked clocks the full pipeline reaches 217.9 images/s, only 1.9\%
more than under the default governor, at the same 74.5\,mJ per image and
0.3\,W more power. Once the pipeline keeps the GPU busy, the stock
governor already selects the top clock. Locking helps the sequential
pipelines by 1.8--2.3$\times$ (2.4$\times$ for the Ultralytics
predictor) and the full pipeline hardly at all.

\textbf{Preprocessing placement interacts with the schedule.}
The float-input variant of the full pipeline in \cref{tab:ladder}
reaches only 89.2 images/s. Host-side
normalization raises process CPU time from 11.6 to 21.6\,ms per image,
the CPU becomes the bottleneck (\cref{eq:ovl}), and the GPU clock
averages only 599\,MHz. In-graph preprocessing is thus worth 18\% in a
sequential pipeline but $2.4\times$ in the full one. Ultralytics 8.4.172
already copies uint8 data and normalizes on the GPU, so the technique is
not new. Our point is that its value depends on whether the schedule
can keep the GPU busy.

\textbf{Where the energy saving comes from.}
Energy per image falls from 194.6\,mJ (Ultralytics) to 74.5\,mJ, a
factor of 2.6. \Cref{eq:energy} shows that most of this saving comes
from amortizing the idle floor (4.68\,W) over more images per second,
not from doing less work per image. The marginal energy above idle is
44.1\,mJ per image for Ultralytics and 52.6\,mJ for the full pipeline.
Across all default-governor rungs it ranges from 39 to 57\,mJ, while the
idle-floor term ranges from 22 to 150\,mJ. The full pipeline does
slightly more work per image above idle (higher clocks, two decoders),
but it finishes much sooner. Its energy advantage is real when the device
works through a backlog or can sleep after finishing. At a fixed camera
rate the situation is different (\cref{sec:stream}).

\subsection{Synchronization: spinning versus sleeping}
\label{sec:sched}

The 20\% effect of the synchronization call is surprising, so we isolated it. By default, \texttt{cudaEventSynchronize} on an event
created without the blocking-sync flag busy-waits, whereas
\texttt{cudaStreamSynchronize} follows the device scheduling policy,
which on this system lets the host thread yield or sleep. We crossed
the two calls with the three device scheduling flags~\cite{nvidia2024cuda}
on the first 2,000 val2017 images (\cref{tab:sched}).

\textbf{Answer to RQ2.}
The decisions that matter are those that change the GPU's duty cycle:
prefetching ($2.9\times$), overlapping post-processing and adding a
decode worker (a further $1.5\times$), and how the host waits for the
GPU. The wait penalty is governor-mediated: on the sequential and
prefetch schedules a sleeping wait lets the CPU governor lower the
host's clock, and through the slower host also the GPU's, and it
nearly vanishes when clocks are locked. On the full two-worker
schedule the wait policy costs nothing at all --- a sleeping event
wait matches a spinning one within 1\% under either governor regime
(\cref{sec:sched}); what still costs 24\% there is the wrong
synchronization boundary (stream synchronization in an enqueue-ahead
loop), identically at locked and default clocks. In-graph preprocessing matters
once the schedule is no longer sequential. Locking clocks substitutes
for a good schedule only partially (1.8--2.3$\times$ for our
sequential pipelines, 2.4$\times$ for the Ultralytics predictor) and
is nearly worthless once the schedule is good (1.9\%). Finally, the
evaluation harness itself can bias the comparison: garbage-collection pauses cost the full pipeline 7.1\% of its measured throughput.
