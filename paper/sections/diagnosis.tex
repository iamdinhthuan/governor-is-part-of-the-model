\section{RQ1: The governor sets the in-application engine time}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

\subsection{Stage attribution in a sequential application}

We first timed every stage of a conventional sequential application
with CUDA events, using the float-input YOLO26n engine at 640, on 1,004
images of the train2017 verification split. The medians were 7.5\,ms
for JPEG decode, 3.9\,ms for letterbox and normalization, 0.65\,ms for
the pinned host-to-device copy, 12.4\,ms for TensorRT execution and
0.01\,ms for the output copy; with row construction and Python-side
dispatch (${\approx}1.5$\,ms), the median total is 26.0\,ms. This
breakdown covers the OpenCV-based row path; the fallback converter
used for the COCO evaluation rows is exercised only by the accuracy
runs and is not in these numbers. The host-stage times are
governor-dependent: this run used the default governors, which held
the CPU domain near 955\,MHz, so the decode and letterbox figures are
those of a down-clocked host. A clocked repeat of the same measurement
(domain logged at 20\,Hz, 837\,MHz mean) reproduced every stage median
within 7\% (decode 8.0\,ms, letterbox 4.2\,ms, compute 12.9\,ms, total
26.9\,ms), so the attribution is stable across the clocks the governor
actually visits. Copies are
negligible on this unified-memory device, and the NMS-free output needs
no suppression. By this account the model takes about half of the
application's time, which would make it the obvious optimization target.

The same engine, however, runs in 4.1\,ms back-to-back in
\texttt{trtexec} (zero gap, two 15\,s runs, medians 4.10 and
4.13\,ms; the uint8-input engine of \cref{tab:dvfs} runs 4.2\,ms).
On val2017, every
sequential pipeline in \cref{tab:ladder} records a mean GPU clock of
exactly 306\,MHz, the governor's floor. Its median enqueue-to-completion time is 13.2--13.3\,ms for both input types, $3.1$--$3.2\times$ the respective back-to-back time.

\subsection{An idle-gap sweep isolates the governor}

To separate the governor from everything else in the application, we
ran the uint8 engines in \texttt{trtexec} with a forced idle gap between
consecutive inferences, for 15\,s per point and twice per point
(\cref{fig:dvfs}, \cref{tab:dvfs}). With locked clocks, YOLO26n's median
compute time is 4.20--4.27\,ms at every gap. With default governors it
stays at 4.2--4.3\,ms up to a 2\,ms gap. It then grows to 5.5\,ms at
4\,ms, 6.8\,ms at 8\,ms, and 12.4\,ms at 33\,ms. Note that
\texttt{--idleTime} inserts host idle time, not a frame period: at a
33\,ms setting the achieved rate is about 26.7 inferences/s, and the
runs carry no camera-style arrival process. Moreover,
\texttt{trtexec} pipelines its enqueues, so the GPU-idle interval the
governor actually sees is shorter than the requested host sleep; the
last row pair of \cref{tab:dvfs} reports the achieved rates, from
which the mean GPU-idle interval can be derived (3.0--25.4\,ms for
YOLO26n against the requested 4--33\,ms). YOLO26s keeps the GPU busy longer per inference, so it
holds its 6.8\,ms up to a 4\,ms gap and rises to 20.5\,ms at 33\,ms.
Read along the idle-gap axis, a host that leaves ${\approx}30$\,ms
between inferences therefore pays about $3\times$ the minimum compute
time per frame under the default governor; actual camera-rate arrival
is measured directly in \cref{sec:stream}.

\subsection{Validating the duty-cycle model}

\textbf{Engine time.}
\Cref{tab:dvfs} compares the measured compute time with the prediction
of \cref{eq:gf}. The prediction uses $G_{\max}$ from the locked runs
(4.22\,ms for YOLO26n, 6.78\,ms for YOLO26s) and the harmonic-mean clock
sampled during each run. Over the 14 down-clocked sweep runs, the mean
absolute error is 4.8\%, and the per-cell errors are signed in both
directions ($-6.7$\% for YOLO26n at the 4\,ms gap to $+11.0$\% for
YOLO26n at the 16\,ms gap).
The largest error (23\%) occurs in one of the
two 16\,ms-gap YOLO26n runs. Its median compute time differs from its
repeat's by 30\%, although their harmonic-mean clocks differ by only
8\%. This suggests clock changes faster than our 20\,Hz sampling
resolves. For the non-overlapped
application rungs of \cref{tab:ladder}, \cref{eq:gf} overestimates the
measured enqueue-to-completion time by 0.4--11.5\%. The positive bias is expected
in part because \cref{eq:gf} attributes all of the engine time to the
GPU clock, leaving no room for a clock-insensitive component
(memory-bound kernels, launch overhead) that a two-term fit
$G(f)=a+b/f$ would absorb. But the approximation itself also
contributes: the window's harmonic-mean clock includes host-only
intervals, during which the clock is lower. Re-weighting the clock by
the driver's per-sample GPU load in the separate load-logging session
reduces the prefetch rungs' overestimate from $+19$--$36$\% to
$+9$--$13$\%, so a large share of the apparent bias where the clock
moves is a sampling artefact of the window average, not physics. The
sequential rungs, pinned at 306\,MHz for the whole run, still show a
$5$--$7$\% overestimate, which is the cleaner estimate of the
clock-insensitive share. We therefore treat \cref{eq:gf} as a first
order description with a ${\sim}5$\% clock-insensitive residual, not as
an exact law.

A fixed-frequency sweep makes the same point without any
window-averaging approximation: with the GPU's devfreq domain pinned
at each of its eight steps (and the CPU and memory clocks left at
their shipped settings), the same uint8 engine at zero gap takes
12.49, 9.54, 7.86, 6.48, 5.68, 5.05, 4.59 and 4.25\,ms at 306, 408,
510, 612, 714, 816, 918 and 1020\,MHz (\cref{tab:fixedfreq}). A pure
inverse fit $G=b/f$ reproduces these medians with a mean absolute
error of 3.7\% and a systematic residual ($+3.1$\% at the floor,
$-9.1$\% at the top clock); the two-term fit $G(f)=a+b/f$ removes the
structure ($a=0.63$\,ms, $b=3.63$\,GHz$\cdot$ms, mean absolute error
0.7\%, all residuals within $\pm1.5$\%). The pinned 306\,MHz time of
12.49\,ms also closes the loop with the application: adding the
${\approx}0.7$\,ms launch path at that clock reproduces the sequential
pipeline's 13.2\,ms enqueue-to-completion.

Because the memory (EMC) clock also scales with load on this SoC, it
is a possible confound for the sweep; prior work reports memory-clock
effects of 11--48\% on edge-inference latency~\cite{kang2026emc}. We
reran the sweep with three repeats per step and the EMC clock logged
at ${\approx}4$\,Hz, plus two control runs with the EMC governor fully
unlocked (an attempted EMC lock did not hold, so we control the
confound by measurement rather than by pinning). The logged traces
show the memory clock bistable: constant 2133\,MHz at GPU clocks of
306--510\,MHz, and alternating 2133--3199\,MHz from 612\,MHz up. Two
checks bound its influence: fitting $a{+}b/f$ to only the
EMC-constant points changes $b$ by 2\% (3.55 versus
3.63\,GHz$\cdot$ms), and the unlocked control runs reproduce the
pinned medians within 0.4\%. The inverse relation is therefore a
GPU-clock effect, not a memory-clock artifact.

\textbf{Governor operating points.}
The busy fraction $u$ of \cref{sec:sysmodel} can be estimated from
timing, but the GPU driver also reports its own load estimate, in
per mille, through a sysfs node. In a separate session we reran each of our
eight pipeline rungs on the first 2,000 val2017 images and logged this load
together with the GPU and CPU clocks at 20\,Hz. The measured load agrees
with the timing estimate to within 0.05 for every rung (the estimate is
0.01--0.05 higher), so we use the measured value for the application
rungs below.

\Cref{fig:operating} plots every operating point as GPU load against
harmonic-mean clock. Whenever the governor settles clearly inside its
range (400--1000\,MHz), the load lies between 0.53 and 0.74: 0.53--0.64 for the \texttt{trtexec} sweep (timing estimate) and 0.64--0.74 for the application rungs (measured). Two caveats bound this band: the
sweep and the application runs use different estimators, with the
timing estimate biased 0.01--0.05 high, so the lower edge cannot be
separated from that bias; and the 0.74 upper edge comes from a rung
settled at 993\,MHz, close to the 1020\,MHz clamp, so the true upper
threshold may lie higher. With those caveats, this is the load-band behavior assumed in
\cref{sec:sysmodel}. The sequential rungs sit at the floor with measured
loads of 0.46--0.66. Even at the floor, their load sits at or below the
observed band's top, which is consistent with the governor never
raising the clock. This is
the fixed point described in \cref{sec:sysmodel}. Removing host stages
from the GPU's critical path pushes the load to the top of the band or
above it, and the clock follows: prefetch with event synchronization settles at a
harmonic-mean clock of 853\,MHz in the load-logging session and
881--891\,MHz across the three backlog repeats (the stream-synchronized
variant of the same rung stays at 655\,MHz, \cref{sec:sched}), and the
full schedule, at a measured load of 0.87 (timing estimate 0.90), stays
at 1020\,MHz.

\textbf{The floor is the only stable point we observe.}
A load-band controller could, in principle, hold a sequential pipeline at
a high clock if it started there. We tested this by starting the
sequential stream-synchronized pipeline with locked clocks and restoring
the default governor after 10\,s, twice. At 1020\,MHz the pipeline's
load was only 0.35--0.36, below the band. Within 0.25\,s of the release
the governor began to step down (1020, 714, 612, 510, 408\,MHz), held
408\,MHz for about 2.5\,s at a load of about 0.5, and reached 306\,MHz
3.3--3.4\,s after the release. It stayed there for the rest of the run
at a load of 0.52--0.53. We find no second operating point in any run.
The load-band model does not exclude interior equilibria in principle
--- a pipeline whose load stops responding to the clock could settle
elsewhere --- but for this workload the release lands at the floor from
every starting clock we tried.

\textbf{Clock and power traces.}
\Cref{fig:traces} shows the first 12\,s of four application runs. Both
sequential variants stay at 306\,MHz for the whole run. Prefetch
climbs in steps from the floor and then moves between about 700 and
1020\,MHz. The full
schedule is pinned at 1020\,MHz. Input power follows the clock. Both
optimized schedules also show short power dips at irregular, on average growing, intervals of 1.9--7.5\,s in the plotted windows. We
traced these dips to Python's cyclic garbage collector, whose pauses grow
as the list of detection rows grows; the stated interval range is measured
over the full runs, so the largest interval extends past the plotted
window. We quantify their effect over
whole runs in \cref{sec:ladder}.

\textbf{Answer to RQ1.}
Under the default governor, the in-application cost of a fixed engine
is set by the application's GPU duty cycle. Engine time follows the
inverse of the clock (\cref{eq:gf}). Every operating point we observe
inside the governor's range sits in a GPU-load band of 0.53--0.74 ---
0.64--0.74 measured by the driver's load counter on the application
runs, 0.53--0.64 from a timing estimate on the engine sweep, biased
0.01--0.05 high --- a band consistent with the load-band behavior of
\cref{sec:sysmodel}, whose exact internal thresholds we do not
identify. The only other state we observe is the floor: a sequential
pipeline reaches it from any starting clock and stays there, which
makes its engine about $3\times$ slower than the benchmark. This
effect is larger than any engine-time change produced by the
compression methods we tried (\cref{sec:model}).
