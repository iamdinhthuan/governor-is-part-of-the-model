\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Real-time object detection on embedded GPUs is usually evaluated with
two numbers per model: accuracy on COCO~\cite{lin2014coco} and a
latency taken from an isolated benchmark of the compiled engine.
Efficiency research then works mainly on the model. Structured
pruning~\cite{li2017pruning,fang2023depgraph},
quantization-aware training~\cite{jacob2018quantization} and
distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling} are judged by how much engine
latency they remove per unit of accuracy lost. Recent NMS-free
detectors make this framing seem even more natural. YOLOv10~\cite{wang2024yolov10}
and YOLO26~\cite{jocher2026yolo26,ultralytics2026yolo26} emit a fixed set of final detections
from a one-to-one head, so the host only decodes the image and converts
a few hundred output rows. With post-processing nearly gone, the engine
benchmark should predict the application.

We find that on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super it does not. The FP16
TensorRT engine of YOLO26n completes an inference in 4.2\,ms when run
back-to-back. Inside a conventional sequential application that
decodes a JPEG, letterboxes it, runs the engine and builds detection
rows, the same engine takes 13.2\,ms. The model, input and builder are
unchanged. What changes is the GPU's duty cycle. While the CPU decodes,
the GPU idles, and the stock frequency governor responds by holding the
GPU at its minimum clock of 306\,MHz, 30\% of its maximum. The
application therefore never runs the model in the regime that the
benchmark measures. This also changes how compression should be
judged: a model that is 20\% cheaper in the benchmark would gain
little in such an application --- at a fixed clock the saving is
diluted by the host stages, and the clock itself does not move.

We did not set out to study governors. We first tried to make
YOLO26n cheaper: object-aware structured pruning, quantization-aware
training (QAT), distillation, compiler-guided pruning of the most expensive
head layers, and a per-image adaptive-resolution router. These variants
either lost several AP points or did not run faster once compiled, and
the router failed verification on held-out images. We then left the
model alone and restructured only the host pipeline. Under the same
default governors and with byte-identical predictions, that change gave
$2.4\times$ the throughput of the best sequential pipeline even when the
latter runs with all clocks locked at maximum, and $6.9\times$ that of
the standard Ultralytics predictor.

This paper is a systematic measurement study of that gap. It is guided
by five research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\RQ{1}] How does the GPU governor's response to an application's
  duty cycle determine the in-application cost of a fixed engine, and
  can a simple model describe it?
\item[\RQ{2}] Which host-pipeline decisions matter for throughput and
  energy, how large is each effect, and which of them interact with the
  governor?
\item[\RQ{3}] How do these decisions trade latency against energy when
  frames arrive at camera rates rather than as a backlog?
\item[\RQ{4}] How do model-level decisions (input resolution,
  compression, model size) rank once the pipeline is fixed?
\item[\RQ{5}] Do the findings carry over to other NMS-free detectors
  and to other accelerator architectures?
\end{itemize}

\noindent Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A duty-cycle model of in-application engine time and energy
  (\cref{sec:sysmodel}), checked against direct measurements
  (\cref{sec:diagnosis}). Engine time scales with the inverse of the
  measured clock. Using the GPU load reported by the driver, we show that
  the measurements are consistent with a load-band governor whose
  observed band is schedule-specific, and that a sequential
  pipeline sits at or below the observed band's top even at
  the minimum clock, which is consistent with why it never leaves the floor. A split of energy into an idle
  floor and a marginal term provides an empirical upper envelope for
  the measured below-capacity streaming cells
  (\cref{sec:stream}).
\item A controlled, cumulative pipeline ablation on all 5,000 COCO
  val2017 images (\cref{sec:ladder}). Scheduling variants produce
  byte-identical predictions, so accuracy is held exactly constant. For
  each variant we report official COCO AP, throughput, input energy, GPU
  clock and CPU time, under both default and locked clocks, and we sweep
  the number of decode workers. Among other effects, we show that the
  choice between two CUDA synchronization calls costs 17--20\% of throughput
  under the default governors. On the sequential and prefetch schedules
  this loss is governor-mediated and drops below 1\% with locked clocks;
  on the full two-worker schedule a 19\% wait penalty remains even with
  clocks locked. A
  controlled test traces the governor-mediated component to the CPU governor: a sleeping wait lets
  \texttt{schedutil} lower the clock of the host's frequency domain.
  Keeping that domain busy recovers 97\% of the loss, and a per-task utilization clamp~\cite{linux2024uclamp}, set without root privileges, recovers most of it at lower energy. We also
  show that garbage-collection pauses in a typical evaluation loop cost a fast pipeline 7.1\% of its measured throughput.
\item A streaming evaluation at 15, 30 and 60\,fps (\cref{sec:stream}).
  It shows that energy per frame is set mainly by idle power, that the
  configuration with the highest throughput adds one frame period of
  latency at low rates, and that an arrival-aware variant removes this
  cost while keeping p95 latency at or below 41\,ms at all three rates.
\item An evaluation of model-level decisions under a fixed, optimized
  pipeline (\cref{sec:model}). Lower input resolution buys 27\% throughput in
  a naive pipeline but only 2--4\% in an optimized one (6--10\% with
  Python's cyclic garbage collector disabled). Our compression and
  routing attempts are reported as negative results. On YOLO26s and
  YOLOv10n, the pipeline effects are comparable in size to YOLO26n's
  ($5.6$--$6.9\times$ over the standard predictor).
\item A replication of the core experiments on two further boards
  (\cref{sec:crossplatform}): a Raspberry Pi~5 with a Hailo-8 NPU, whose
  accelerator has no user-visible frequency control, and a Rubik Pi~3
  (Qualcomm QCM6490) measured twice, on its CPU and on its Hexagon NPU.
  The pipeline ladder transfers ($3.3\times$ on the Pi~5 with
  byte-identical predictions, $2.7\times$ before the wait-policy
  change), while the Jetson-specific accelerator-governor signature is
  not directly observed on the NPU stacks, where the identifiable
  mechanisms shift to the host side; on CPU
  inference the ladder flattens and sustained load becomes thermally
  limited, and on the NPU stack the CPU governor becomes the largest
  term (57\% on the matched overlap rungs), because the scheduler down-clocks the core that feeds
  the accelerator (from 2.7\,GHz pinned to 0.95--1.8\,GHz, depending
  on how little host work the schedule leaves on the critical path).
\end{enumerate}

We do not propose a new detector, compression method or governor, and
pipelining itself is standard practice. The contribution is the
measurement and its explanation. On this class of device, the
interaction between the host pipeline and the frequency governor
determines more of the end-to-end cost than the model-level choices
that usually receive the attention. We provide all scripts, raw power
traces and prediction hashes, so that the results can be checked and
reproduced (see the data and code availability statement).
