\section{RQ3: Latency and energy at camera rates}
\label{sec:stream}

A camera delivers frames at a fixed rate rather than as a backlog. The
first 1,000 val2017 images were therefore released at 15, 30 and
60\,fps, and for each frame the latency from release to finished
detection rows and the module input energy were measured
(\cref{tab:stream,fig:stream}). The arrival-aware variant
(\cref{sec:pipelines}) is included; on a backlog it is 8.9\% slower than
the full pipeline (\cref{tab:ladder}).

\textbf{Capacity.}
Pipelines slower than the arrival rate accumulate a growing queue. At
60\,fps these are the Ultralytics predictor (31.0 images/s achieved), the float sequential
pipeline (34.2) and the uint8 sequential pipeline with event
synchronization (49.7; rounded in \cref{tab:stream}). The Ultralytics
predictor is also near capacity at 30\,fps (29.8 images/s). Short
stalls there create slowly draining queues, so its latency is strongly
right-skewed (mean 55--64\,ms against medians of 36--37\,ms). Arrivals
are anchored at the end of the harness warm-up; anchoring them earlier
would inflate this cell's p95 from 188 to 447\,ms. Dropping the first
100 frames of every run moves the below-capacity p95 values by at most
4\,ms (188 to 192\,ms for this cell), so the remaining tails are
steady-state queue drain, not start-up transients. Backlog capacity
thus matters even for a camera: it is the margin that absorbs transient
stalls.

\textbf{Energy is set by the idle floor.}
At 15\,fps every pipeline uses between 352 and 361\,mJ per frame, of
which the idle floor alone is 312\,mJ (86--89\%). At 30\,fps the spread
is 196--204\,mJ against a floor of 156\,mJ. On a backlog the full
pipeline uses 2.6$\times$ less energy per image than the Ultralytics
predictor, but at 30\,fps it uses only 2.5\% less (195.7 versus
200.7\,mJ). Only at 60\,fps, where the slow pipelines saturate, does
the schedule decide energy again: 117--123\,mJ per processed frame for
the pipelines that keep up, against 151--194\,mJ for those that do not.

\Cref{eq:stream} describes this behavior. With the backlog idle power
and marginal energy and the arrival rate substituted, it agrees with
every below-capacity streaming cell within $-0.2$ to $+11.6$\% (mean $+4.6$\%). The two slightly negative cells (the Ultralytics predictor at 15 and
30\,fps, $-0.2$\% each) lie inside the repeat spread of the backlog marginal estimate.
For the three pipelines that saturate at 60\,fps, the arrival-rate
substitution underestimates by 10--37\%, because a queued pipeline never
returns to the idle state the model assumes; substituting the measured
processed-frame rate (as the published aggregates do) reduces the error
to 0.3--0.5\%.

The agreement comes mostly from the idle term, which
dominates at camera rates. The marginal term alone is overestimated by
21--39\% for the uint8 pipelines below capacity but agrees within
$-1.9$ to $+3.4$\% for the float-input sequential pipeline and the
Ultralytics predictor (which normalizes on the GPU, \cref{sec:ladder}). The backlog-to-stream clock gap does not explain the
uint8 discrepancy: the worst cell (the event-synchronized sequential
pipeline at 15\,fps, $+39$\%) runs at 306\,MHz in both. The residue
cannot be attributed from these aggregates, since CPU sleep states and
any inter-frame GPU power gating differ between the workloads and the
module rail does not separate them. The backlog marginal energy is not a prediction of the streaming value but an
empirical upper envelope for it in every measured uint8 configuration,
and lies within $-2$ to $+4$\% of it for the other two pipelines. At 15
and 30\,fps the GPU runs at about 306\,MHz in all pipelines, against
about 1\,GHz for the optimized pipelines on the backlog, so marginal
energy per frame is lower: the governor does what it is designed to do.

Locking clocks with \texttt{jetson\_clocks} raises the idle floor from
4.68 to 6.62\,W, which at 30\,fps is 221\,mJ per frame
before any work, already 8--13\% more than the total energy of every
default-governor pipeline in \cref{tab:stream}.

\textbf{Latency is set by the schedule.}
At low rates the overlapped schedule, despite the highest backlog
throughput, has the worst latency: a median of
85.3\,ms at 15\,fps, against 27.4\,ms for the sequential pipeline.
Overlap finishes the rows of frame $i$ only after frame $i{+}1$ has
been decoded and enqueued, so every result waits about one
inter-arrival period (66.7\,ms at 15\,fps), a penalty that shrinks as
the rate grows (\cref{tab:stream}).
The sequential pipeline is fastest per frame at 30\,fps (median 21.6,
p95 25.8\,ms), where its backlog capacity of ${\approx}50$\,images/s
exceeds the arrival rate by two thirds, but it fails at 60\,fps.
Plain prefetch keeps up at 60\,fps, but its p95 rises to 99.3\,ms: the
governor runs it at about 410\,MHz, so its margin over the arrival rate
is small and transient stalls build queues.

The sequential pipeline's per-frame cost also depends on the arrival
rate: for the same work, its median latency (27.4 versus 21.6\,ms) and
CPU time per frame (29.4 versus 23.5\,ms) are both higher at 15 than at
30\,fps. The logged clock of the fastest core (1705\,MHz at 15\,fps,
1682\,MHz at 30\,fps) does not explain this. A per-core effect on the
waiting thread's core remains possible, but the measured clocks do not support the
down-clocking mechanism of \cref{sec:sched} here; the extra CPU time is
at least as consistent with per-frame sleep/wake overhead.

The arrival-aware variant combines the two behaviors, finishing the
in-flight frame when no next frame is available and overlapping when
frames queue up. Its p95
latency is within 7.4\,ms of the best pipeline at every rate, it is the
only pipeline whose p95 stays at or below 41\,ms at all three rates
(\cref{tab:stream}), and its energy is within 0.4--4.3\% of the full
pipeline. Its predictions are
byte-identical to those of the other uint8 pipelines.

\textbf{Answer to RQ3.}
At camera rates below a pipeline's capacity, energy per frame is
dominated by the idle floor: the tested pipelines differ by at most
2.5\% at 15\,fps and 4.2\% at 30\,fps. The schedule mainly determines
latency and capacity margin: the one with the best backlog throughput
has the worst latency at 15\,fps because it holds every
result for one frame period. An arrival-aware schedule removes this
penalty at a cost of 8.9\% of backlog throughput and at most 4.3\% of
energy. Locking clocks raises the idle floor and is counterproductive
in this regime.
