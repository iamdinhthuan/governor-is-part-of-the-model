\textbf{The effect is spinning versus sleeping.}
In the sequential schedule, event synchronization gives 49.9--50.0
images/s under all three flags. Stream synchronization gives 40.5 images/s
with \texttt{auto} and 40.0 with \texttt{blocking-sync}, but 50.0 with
\texttt{spin}. The effect (19.8\% on the ladder rungs of \cref{tab:ladder}
and 16.7\% in the pinned-CPU experiment below) thus depends only on
whether the waiting host thread spins or sleeps, not on events versus
streams. On this stack, \texttt{cudaEventSynchronize} spun
under every device flag, consistent with the CUDA documentation, which
ties blocking event waits to the \texttt{cudaEventBlockingSync} creation
flag~\cite{nvidia2024cuda}; the events used here do not set it.
Spinning costs 3.7--4.2\,ms of process CPU time
per image and 1.2\,W of module input power, yet energy per image is 4\%
lower (151.1 versus 157.2\,mJ) because each image finishes sooner.

\textbf{Engine time is unchanged; host work slows down.}
The GPU clock is 306\,MHz and the engine's enqueue-to-completion time
is 13.2--13.3\,ms in all six sequential cells, so the waking thread does
not measurably miss the completion. (The span is not pure GPU time:
with the host clamped at 115\,MHz it grows to 31.5\,ms, as it then
contains the host's launch path.) The difference lies
in the host stages. The per-image period grows from 20.0 to
24.7--25.0\,ms, and the sampled clock of the fastest CPU core falls from
1728\,MHz to about 1643--1694\,MHz. The suspected cause is the CPU
governor (\texttt{schedutil}), which clocks each frequency domain in
proportion to the estimated utilization of its
CPUs~\cite{linux2024cpufreq}. While the host thread sleeps through the
13\,ms engine phase, its core appears idle and the domain is
down-clocked, so the decode, letterbox and row stages of the next image
run at the lower clock.

\textbf{A controlled test.}
Without changing the governor, the sequential pipeline was pinned to
CPU 0, and the clock of its frequency domain (CPUs 0--3) was logged at
20\,Hz (\cref{tab:cpufreq}). With stream synchronization the domain
averaged 62\% of its maximum clock. A separate busy-loop process on
CPU 1, doing no pipeline work, holds the shared domain at 1728\,MHz.
Throughput then rose to 49.0 images/s, closing 97\% of the gap to event
synchronization (49.2 images/s), and the pipeline's own CPU time fell. The busy loop had no
effect on the event-synchronized pipeline, which already held the
maximum clock. One confound remains: a busy loop also keeps the
cluster out of deep idle states, which shortens wake-up latency. Clamping the whole domain at its 115\,MHz minimum, where the
governor cannot respond to the loop, bounds this confound. The loop then
changes nothing (7.6 versus 7.7\,images/s, under 1\%, mean of two
runs), so its $+20$\% at the shipped governor is a clock effect. The
clamp test cannot rule out sub-millisecond idle-state or wake-up effects
at operating clocks, because at 115\,MHz a microsecond-scale cost is
invisible next to 130\,ms frames. The \texttt{uclamp} experiment below, however, acts
on frequency alone and recovers about 87\% of the loss, which bounds the
idle-state share to at most 13\%. A spinning wait keeps the CPU clock
high as a side effect.

\textbf{An unprivileged remedy.}
A busy loop is a diagnostic, not a fix. Utilization
clamping~\cite{linux2024uclamp} tells \texttt{schedutil} directly that a
task needs a high clock. A task can raise its own \texttt{uclamp.min}
with \texttt{sched\_setattr}, without root privileges on the test
system, and threads it creates inherit the clamp. Setting
\texttt{uclamp.min} of the stream-synchronized pipeline to its maximum
(1024), with no other change, raised the domain clock to 1450\,MHz and
throughput from 41.3 to 48.0 images/s (\cref{tab:cpufreq}, bottom). Against
the same-session event-synchronized reference (49.0 images/s), the clamp
closes about 87\% of the sleeping-wait penalty. The recovery is partial
because the clamp acts only while the task is runnable, not while it
sleeps through the engine phase. Module input
power rose by only 0.48\,W, so energy per image fell by 7\%, from 153.2
to 142.0\,mJ. This is below both the spinning wait (151.1--151.3\,mJ)
and the busy loop (157\,mJ), because the clamp raises the clock without
burning a core. Per-task clamping is thus a practical alternative
to spinning when a sleeping wait is required.

\textbf{The two governors compound.}
With prefetch, sleeping waits reduce throughput from about 141 to
107--111 images/s, a 21--24\% loss. The slower host leaves the GPU idle
for longer, the GPU governor settles at 674--694 instead of
860--866\,MHz, and the engine time rises from 4.6 to 6.1--6.3\,ms. A
drop in the CPU clock thus lowers the GPU clock as well. This
antagonism has been described for the stock governors of the same board
family~\cite{zhang2026sparsedvfs}: while the GPU computes, the host
looks idle and is down-clocked, so it runs slowly exactly when the GPU
next needs data. The present measurements trace the trigger to the CUDA
wait policy, bound the effect with two controls, test an unprivileged
per-task remedy, and show its propagation to the GPU clock through the
duty cycle.

\textbf{The clamp also lifts the GPU clock.}
If the compounding runs from the host clock to the GPU clock,
re-arming the CPU governor should restore both. \texttt{uclamp.min} was
therefore set to 1024 on the prefetch rung, where the sleeping wait
costs the most (\cref{tab:uclamp2}, two repeats per cell). With
blocking synchronization, the clamp raises the feeder cores' clock from
1615--1620 to 1714--1716\,MHz. Faster submission raises the GPU's duty
cycle, and the GPU governor settles at 827--860 instead of
631--657\,MHz, the range of the spinning and event-synchronized
variants. Engine time falls from 6.3--6.9 to 4.7\,ms, and throughput
rises 34\% (101.8--104.2 to 136.3--140.2 images/s) at unchanged energy
per image (84.3 to 85.2--86.7\,mJ). The clamp thus recovers essentially
all of prefetch's sleeping-wait penalty without burning a core.

\textbf{On the full pipeline the wait policy is free.}
\label{sec:waitfree}
The full two-worker schedule is simpler (\cref{tab:uclamp2}, bottom).
With the decode pool active, a \emph{sleeping} event wait (per-frame
events created with \texttt{cudaEventBlockingSync}) reaches
215.5--215.9 images/s, indistinguishable from the spinning reference
(215.3--215.6), with 0.3\,ms less process CPU time and 0.4--0.5\,mJ less
energy per image. Locking the clocks changes neither arm (215.1--215.6
sleeping, 214.7--215.5 spinning), and the clamp has nothing left to
recover (215.0--215.5). Here the host never goes idle on the critical
path. While the main thread sleeps through one completion, two decode
workers keep the domain busy, so the governor sees no utilization dip.
The wait policy matters only where a single thread sleeps through each
engine phase: the sequential and prefetch rungs. A GIL control on the
same schedule (switch interval shortened tenfold, 5 to 0.5\,ms, under
locked clocks) also changes nothing: 215.0--215.8 versus 215.1--215.6
images/s.

\textbf{Stream synchronization costs a quarter through its boundary,
not its wait.}
Blocking \emph{stream} synchronization in the enqueue-ahead loop still
loses 24\% (163.9--165.1 versus
215.3--215.9 images/s), and the same 24\% with locked clocks
(163.9--164.7), where the GPU stays at 1020\,MHz. The cost thus comes
from neither the sleeping wait nor the governor, but from the completion
boundary. \texttt{cudaStreamSynchronize} waits for \emph{all} work in
the stream, including the frame just enqueued, so the rows of frame
$i{-}1$ are finished only after frame $i$ has executed. The loop
degenerates to in-order completion with one frame of slack, and the
enqueue-to-completion window stretches from 7.2 to 10.0\,ms. The
per-frame event, spinning or sleeping, preserves the overlap.

\textbf{Locking clocks removes the governor-mediated penalty where
there is one.}
\texttt{jetson\_clocks} fixes the CPU clock at its maximum as well as
the GPU clock. On the sequential and prefetch schedules, stream and
event synchronization then differ by less than 1\% (\cref{tab:ladder}),
so the roughly 20\% penalty on these schedules is governor-mediated. On
the full two-worker schedule, sleeping and spinning event waits agree
within 1\% at both clock settings, and only the stream-sync boundary
(above) costs throughput, identically at both.

The contrast between schedules therefore depends on the governor. The
full two-worker pipeline outperforms the event-synchronized sequential
pipeline by $4.28\times$ under the default governors (213.8 versus
50.0 images/s, \cref{tab:ladder}) but only by $2.41\times$ with locked
clocks (217.9 versus 90.4). Locking does not raise the overlapped
pipeline's ceiling; it removes the sequential baseline's
self-inflicted penalty. The reported benefit of scheduling thus depends
on the benchmark's power-management regime.
