Channel pruning removed 25\% of the output channels of four backbone
convolutions, 5.6\% of the parameters. Our object-risk criterion
scores channels by gradient-weighted foreground activations and a
fake-INT8 perturbation proxy, and it kept 2.7 AP points more than L1
magnitude pruning at the same budget. Both pruned models still lost 6.8--9.5
AP points. Neither was faster after compilation, because the pruned
early convolutions are not where TensorRT spends its time. Ultralytics QAT
produced a mixed-precision engine (about half of the convolutions in
INT8, the rest FP16/FP32 behind reformatting layers). It was slower than
FP16 under both builder levels. Distillation from the fine-tuned teacher
reduced AP in both pruning settings (L1: 0.369 to 0.338; object-risk:
0.395 to 0.373 on the development split). We also pruned the parts that
per-layer profiles showed to be expensive (P3/P4 classification-head
channels and the attention block's feed-forward width). We found no
stable change in engine time beyond run-to-run noise (about 6.5\,ms
back-to-back with \texttt{opt0} builds; the table's 21.8\,ms column is
the same engine inside a sequential application, and the difference
between the two is the governor effect of \cref{sec:diagnosis}). These results come from a short recovery budget ---
one seed, five epochs, \texttt{opt0} builds on one device. With
longer training, the pruned models would likely recover more AP, and
a full \texttt{opt3} build sweep might redistribute the engine time
differently. They do
not show that compression cannot work. They show something narrower:
for a 2.6\,M-parameter NMS-free detector on this device and budget,
the achievable engine-time savings are a small fraction of the
3$\times$ governor effect of \cref{sec:diagnosis}.

\subsection{The per-image resolution router}
\label{app:router}

We fit a logistic router on cheap pre-inference image statistics. It
chooses a 512 or 640 engine per image, and its threshold was fixed to
send 20\% of images to 640. On its 512-image held-out half, it slightly
beat a fixed 576 engine in both AP and median time. We then froze it and evaluated it once on an independent, hash-verified 1,024-image train2017 split that played no part in fitting or threshold selection (\cref{sec:setup}). Against fixed 576 it lost 0.0089 AP (0.4386 vs.\
0.4476) and 0.0147 AP$_S$, and both 512-image halves lost AP. Its
median application time was 1.1--1.4\,ms lower across three paired
runs (41.3--41.9 versus 42.4--43.2\,ms), but its
95th-percentile time was not. Small-object recall at IoU 0.5 fell by
1.55 points (paired image bootstrap 95\% interval $[-2.98, -0.19]$).
We therefore rejected it. In the same units, the scheduling steps of
\cref{tab:ladder} save 15--28\,ms of median per-image latency depending
on the starting pipeline; the router's ${\approx}1.2$\,ms is at most
one eleventh of that. The comparison is in latency rather than
period: the router was evaluated in a sequential pipeline, so its
saving does not compose with an overlapped schedule.

\section{QNN userspace notes}
\label{app:qnn}

Two findings about the bundled \texttt{qai-appbuilder}/QNN userspace
(QAIRT 2.50.40 wheel on QCM6490, V68 HTP) shaped the HTP campaign.
First, it is not safe under concurrent inference: issuing calls from
two contexts at once wedges the DSP session after a few hundred calls
(an empty output or a session that never returns; retries and context
rebuilds do not recover it). We can only speak for this bundled
userspace as shipped --- the vendor SDK advertises multi-context
support, which we did not exercise. Our pipeline therefore holds
exactly one context and issues every NPU call from a single thread;
overlap comes from pipelining decode, input fill and host NMS
\emph{around} the serialized NPU calls. Under that discipline the
whole campaign ran without a single failed call.

Second, one harness bug is worth recording. An earlier version of the
evaluation script passed the input buffer to the appbuilder wrapper
unflattened, and the wrapper's reshape helper caught the resulting
broadcast error and printed it on every call, inside the timed region
(${\approx}1$\,ms per call at the shipped governor, more at pinned low
clocks; predictions were unaffected --- the native call received the
correct buffer). We fixed the call, reran the entire HTP campaign, and
report only the corrected numbers; the fix moved the shipped-governor
rungs by ${\approx}1$\,ms per call, the \texttt{performance} ladder by
up to 13\% (the pinned core now spends its cycles on dispatch instead
of printing), and the 806\,MHz probe point by 8\,ms per call, so
it mattered most exactly where the paper looks at clock sensitivity.
