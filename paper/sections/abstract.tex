\begin{abstract}
The deployment cost of NMS-free detectors is usually equated with
engine latency. Here, the full decode-to-detections pipeline of YOLO26n
is measured on a Jetson Orin Nano Super, with module input power,
clocks, GPU load and COCO accuracy on the 5,000 val2017 images. The
FP16 TensorRT engine that takes 4.2\,ms back-to-back takes 13.2\,ms
inside a sequential application, because the stock GPU governor holds
the GPU at its minimum clock. A duty-cycle model describes the
measurements. Engine time scales with the inverse of the clock (3.7\%
mean absolute error, fitted on a fixed-frequency sweep),
interior-clock operating points lie in an observed GPU-load range of
0.53--0.74, and a sequential pipeline released from the top clock
falls to the floor within seconds. Restructuring only the host
pipeline, under default governors and with identical predictions,
gives $2.4\times$ the throughput of the best sequential pipeline at
locked maximum clocks. A sleeping CUDA wait also lets
\texttt{schedutil} lower the host clock and, through slower
submission, the GPU clock. A per-task utilization clamp recovers most
of this loss without busy-spinning. On the full pipeline, sleeping and
spinning waits perform alike under default and locked clocks. Below
capacity, idle power sets the energy per frame and the schedule sets
the latency. Replications on a Raspberry Pi~5 with a Hailo-8 NPU and
on a Qualcomm QCM6490 show that the effect transfers, while its
mechanism moves to the host. Edge detection efficiency should be
reported for the whole pipeline under deployment governors.
\end{abstract}
